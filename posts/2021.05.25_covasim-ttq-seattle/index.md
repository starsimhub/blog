> **TL;DR.** In the first half of 2020, COVID-19 control in the United States rested on broad mobility restrictions: school closures, workplace closures, shelter-in-place orders. This paper, built on Covasim, an agent-based model calibrated to the first six months of the Seattle epidemic, showed that an aggressive test-trace-quarantine (TTQ) program could maintain epidemic control even under a full return to baseline mobility, given strong performance on testing rates, tracing rates, isolation compliance, and turnaround delays. The model's projections were validated against the actual epidemic trajectory in Seattle from June through August 2020. Five years on, Covasim has been generalized into the Starsim framework. The modular contact-layer architecture, the separable intervention design, and the calibration discipline laid out in this paper became the template for the family of agent-based models that followed (HIVsim, HPVsim, STIsim, FPsim).

**Key numbers**
- Approximately 100,000 SARS-CoV-2 infections estimated in Seattle and King County between 27 January and 9 June 2020 (95% confidence interval: 80,000 to 115,000), against 8,548 diagnosed cases
- Attack rate (cumulative infections divided by population): 3.5 to 5.1%
- Effective reproduction number (R_e) dropped from 2.3 prior to interventions to below 1 once the shelter-in-place order took effect
- Transmission overdispersion: 10% of infected individuals were responsible for 50% of subsequent transmissions; 53% caused no secondary infections
- Source of transmission by symptom status: 54% from symptomatic individuals, 32% asymptomatic, 14% presymptomatic
- Transmission by contact layer before school closures: community 32%, household 26%, workplace 26%, school 14%, long-term care facility (LTCF) 1%
- Transmission by contact layer after the stay-at-home order: household 46%, community 28%, workplace 24%, school 2%
- Estimated effect of TTQ delays: a one-day delay in returning test results adds roughly 4 additional infections per person tested over a 90-day window; a one-day tracing delay adds roughly 2 additional infections per index case

**Source.** Kerr CC, Mistry D, Stuart RM, Rosenfeld K, Hart GR, Núñez RC, Cohen JA, Selvaraj P, Abeysuriya RG, Jastrzębski M, et al. (2021). *Controlling COVID-19 via test-trace-quarantine*. Nature Communications 12:2993. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-23276-9). Code at [InstituteforDiseaseModeling/covasim](https://github.com/InstituteforDiseaseModeling/covasim).

## Motivation

When we did this work in early-to-mid 2020, the United States and most other countries were managing COVID-19 through broad, mobility-based non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs): school closures, workplace closures, shelter-in-place orders. These were effective at reducing transmission but carried enormous societal and economic costs. The pressing question was whether a more targeted strategy, built on testing, contact tracing, and quarantine, could maintain epidemic control as restrictions lifted.

Several prior modeling studies had explored test-trace-quarantine (TTQ) in different contexts. What was missing was a detailed, data-driven, agent-based analysis grounded in a specific setting, calibrated to real epidemic data, and capable of disentangling the contributions of mobility restrictions from the contributions of testing and tracing programs themselves. Seattle and King County, Washington, was a natural setting: it was where the first documented COVID-19 case and the first death in the United States occurred, and the local response had been comparatively well-instrumented.

## What we did

We built Covasim, an agent-based model of COVID-19. Agents are represented in five contact layers (household, school, workplace, community, and long-term care facility) with empirically calibrated structures generated by SynthPops from census, school enrolment, and workplace data. Within-host viral dynamics produce variable infectivity by symptom status and time from exposure. Interventions, including symptomatic and asymptomatic testing, contact tracing, isolation, quarantine, masks, and distancing, are implemented as separable, composable modules.

We calibrated Covasim to age-stratified COVID-19 diagnosis and death data from Seattle and King County between 27 January and 9 June 2020, using SafeGraph mobility data for the workplace and community layers and Optuna for hyperparameter optimization. The calibrated model accurately reproduced cumulative diagnoses, cumulative deaths, and the age distribution of cases.

Onto that baseline we layered TTQ scenarios across six aspects of the program: isolation and quarantine effectiveness, routine testing rates, household and workplace contact tracing probabilities, proportions of contacts tested after tracing, test-result return delays, and contact tracing delays. We then asked how each aspect contributed to attack rate under three mobility scenarios (60%, 80%, and 100% return to baseline) and three transmission settings (basic reproduction number R_0 of 2.0, 2.5, and 3.1).

## What we found

The calibration alone produced findings worth reporting. The first six months of the Seattle epidemic involved roughly 100,000 SARS-CoV-2 infections, an order of magnitude more than the 8,548 cases diagnosed by 9 June. Transmission was highly overdispersed: 10% of infected people accounted for half of all subsequent transmissions, while 53% caused no secondary infections at all. Around 54% of transmissions were from symptomatic individuals, with the rest split between asymptomatic and presymptomatic sources.

The school closures and stay-at-home order redistributed transmission dramatically. Before schools closed, community, household, and workplace each carried roughly a quarter of transmissions, with school at 14% and LTCFs at 1%. After the stay-at-home order, household transmission grew to 46% while school transmission dropped to 2%.

On the TTQ question, the central finding was that an aggressive TTQ program could maintain R_e at or below 1 even under a full return to baseline mobility, given strong performance across all six aspects of the program. The contribution of each aspect was material. A one-day delay in returning test results was estimated to add roughly 4 additional infections per person tested over a 90-day window. A one-day delay in tracing added roughly 2 additional infections per traced index case. No single aspect of the program could compensate for severe weakness in another: epidemic control required strong performance across all aspects simultaneously.

When mobility restrictions in Seattle were lifted in June 2020 and testing and tracing were subsequently scaled up, the actual epidemic trajectory through August closely matched the model's projections. The real-world validation lent direct credibility to both the calibration and the TTQ analysis.

## What this set in motion

Covasim was originally developed for this Seattle analysis. By the time the paper appeared in 2021, the same codebase had already been adapted for COVID-19 modeling in more than a dozen other settings (China, Vietnam, Australia, the United Kingdom, and many others). The architectural choices that made that portability possible, namely modular contact layers, separable intervention modules, agent-level state, and support for co-transmission, are the design principles that were later generalized into the Starsim framework. HIVsim, HPVsim, FPsim, and STIsim are all built on that lineage. Covasim itself is now a Starsim family member.

Looking back, several decisions in this paper aged well. Agent-based modeling with detailed contact-layer structure was at the time more involved than most epidemic models attempted; it has since become the norm for the line of work that grew out of Covasim. The combination of calibration to real longitudinal data, mechanistic within-host representation, and explicit modeling of interventions as separable components has carried through largely unchanged into the broader Starsim ecosystem.

The "high but achievable" framing for TTQ requirements also held up empirically. New Zealand, South Korea, Vietnam, and Australia maintained COVID-19 control in 2020 using broadly the components the modeling identified as necessary. Many other settings did not, for reasons that map onto the program aspects the paper had flagged as critical: testing capacity, tracing capacity, isolation compliance, and turnaround delays.

## Caveats

The model assumed county-wide intervention parameters and did not represent geographic clustering or day-of-week mobility variation within the metropolitan area. Susceptibility and transmissibility heterogeneity by age and comorbidity were parameterized using the best evidence available in mid-2020 and carried substantial uncertainty. Several core features of SARS-CoV-2 transmission, including the proportion of asymptomatic and presymptomatic transmission and the effect of masking, were still being characterized when the analysis was performed; the paper handled this by propagating parameter uncertainty into the scenario outputs.

The TTQ scenarios assumed no pharmaceutical interventions and modest mask efficacy and compliance. The arrival of vaccines from late 2020 and the subsequent emergence of variants substantially changed the parameter space the paper analyzed. The findings about TTQ as a containment strategy remain accurate as a description of what was required for control absent vaccines, but their relevance for current policy is largely historical.

## Where this fits

This paper is the foundational publication for what became the Starsim family of agent-based disease models. The substantive scientific findings about TTQ as a containment strategy informed real-world policy responses in 2020 and 2021. The methodological choices, namely agent-level state, modular interventions, detailed contact networks, and calibration to longitudinal data, became the architectural template for the broader family of models that followed.
